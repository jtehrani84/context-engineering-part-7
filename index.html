Three Planes, One Frozen Model

01 · operatethe harness runs without me

A fleet of scheduled jobs (12 defined, 4 loaded now) gathers overnight from Slack, X, news, and GitHub, synthesizes the morning brief, closes the action plan, reseeds grounding, and prunes memory. The "7 AM boundary" from Part 6 became a running daemon. 19 dated intel digests sit on disk, newest stamped today.

02 · verifythe harness checks the truth of its own claims

Part 6's lesson was knowledge needs enforcement, but what it enforced was code shape. Today four sibling rules, each born from a dated failure in real customer or account work (sanitized on this page) and each wired to a gate, enforce something harder than shape. They check that a claim is actually true, so don't say "done" without a running artifact, don't act on a finding the artifact has already outgrown, don't outrun the source you're citing, and don't call something false just because you checked the wrong place. Two of those last four run below.

03 · evolvethe harness improves itself, gated

Salesforce AI Research published DarwinX and open-sourced the framework, Beagle. The idea, in one line, is to make an agent better without retraining. Freeze the model, evolve the harness by selection, and keep a change only if it improves and doesn't regress. I ran it, then turned that discipline inward, so my voice guard is now scored on a held-out corpus and gated. That gate is the second thing you can run below.

Cutting across all three planes is a connector fabric of ~28 MCP servers configured. That's the reach that lets the planes touch real systems, and it's Part 3 grown up.

The Preserve-and-Extend Gate

This is how the harness admits a change to itself, borrowed from DarwinX. A proposed change is scored on a tune set and a held-out set. Admit it only if it gains on tune and doesn't regress the held-out set past a small budget. Drag the sliders, then try to sneak through a change that looks great on average but quietly breaks the held-out set.

What the gate gates

The Same Gate, on Real Ability

The slider you just dragged isn't a toy, it's wired to a real fitness signal built from four published methods. An applied-AI scientist at Amazon pointed me at them, and they map cleanly onto the DarwinX loop. Meta-Knowledge prepares the context, VERA scores the output with an honest bound, ECHO attributes which step failed, and DEEVO optimizes where there's no gold answer. Score a change, gate it, and keep it only if it actually improves.

That loop runs on two surfaces. One is the voice guard from the first demo, small-n, my own labeled corpus. The other is code, where the labels number in the hundreds and the bound is statistically real. That surface is a full SWE-bench Verified rollout through Salesforce AI Research's Beagle/DarwinX framework, on my own GCP.

SWE-bench Verified · the coding gate baseline
88.8% pass@1
95% bootstrap CI [86.0%, 91.4%]
n=500 · no-hints · 444 resolved

And the bounds that keep it honest, because this is the number an expert will probe. What's being scored is stock mini-swe, and no harness lifts a model to 88.8%, so this isn't a claim about my harness at all. It's a 2026 model run against a 2023 public benchmark, so contamination is baked in. That makes it a gate baseline, where a harness change earns its place only when its lower bound beats this, and it doesn't belong on a leaderboard. VERA earns its cite as the method here, fold-to-one-score with an honest bound, the discipline behind how the 88.8% is reported.

The scope is worth being plain about. The selection half is live. Propose a change, score it on both surfaces, and admit it only through the gate. The generation half, an evolver that writes its own candidate changes, I still drive by hand. So this is eval-and-gate; the machine doesn't write its own changes yet, and "evolving" is the present tense on purpose.

Where These Came From

I didn't design the verify plane. It's scar tissue.

Every rule behind those demos was forced by real customer work going wrong in a specific, dated way. A capability claim in a customer Support handoff got written toward the reassuring version instead of what the source actually said. An account audit called a batch of real facts "fabricated" because it checked them against a filing that would never list them (that's the check you just ran, sanitized, it was a real account, and 13 of 15 "fabrications" turned out to be true). An account brief invented dollar figures when the data regenerated underneath it. The account-research platform quietly wrote zero rows for six weeks while every code review called it done.

Three of those four are customer-facing artifacts. The harness learned to check the truth of a claim because a claim reached a customer, or nearly did, and was wrong. Each failure became a rule, and each rule became a wired gate. The tidy "four rules born from a dated failure" line up in the verify plane has a concrete origin behind it. Real deal work went sideways on me first, and each rule is just what I built afterward so that particular failure would stop happening.

It's where the other two planes live too. Operate, in a deal, means every product claim gets grounded in a cited corpus before it reaches a customer, and opening an account's Slack channel or writing into a deal folder auto-fires the reminder to do it. Verify means a territory number traces to one source of record behind a gate that turns red on a wrong figure. This is how the account work gets done now, because of how it used to break.

The caveat is that I can show you the rules and their dated origins, but the deal domain they guard is private, so what's here is the pattern rather than the accounts themselves. And whether every gate fires on a live brief this morning is something I'd prove by running it, rather than by showing you a file.

Where This Is Thin

The self-verify half is mature and load-bearing, with four rules, four wired gates, and use in every session. The self-evolve half is real but early, with one measured guard, one benchmark task the agent actually missed (the gate correctly rejected it), and correction loops that mostly propose rather than act. So the title says "evolving," in the present tense, because it isn't "evolved" yet.

The verifier is only a proxy that scores slop and voice-law. Whether the writing is true or persuasive is beyond it. Optimizing any proxy hard enough eventually games it, which is exactly why the held-out gate exists and why the guard earns "better at catching my known tells" instead of "objectively better writing."

The sharpest gap is that until the day I wrote this, the tooling that makes all of this measurable had no version history, and the folder wasn't even tracked. A harness that preaches "prove it, and don't trust a stale finding" wasn't applying either rule to itself, which is fixed now. And it's the most honest thing in this whole series, because the drift you saw in the last demo is just the system's natural entropy, and you don't hold entropy off with faith, you hold it off by auditing your own machine on a schedule, as adversarially as you'd audit anyone else's.

The ceiling

I Tried to Grade My Own Writing

The evolve plane already scores my writing for AI tells, and that's the guard from the first demo. So I asked whether it could catch AI pretending to be me. I built the test aimed straight at the guard's blind spot, answering the same questions in my own register, lowercase, with my phrasing and my analogies. Call each of these before you read the numbers.

Spot the mimic · human or machine?

Three short answers to Salesforce questions, each written by an AI told to answer in my voice. A person, or a machine imitating one?

The consensus of three language-model judges caught obvious AI 11 times out of 13, and never once flagged my real writing as fake. Against AI wearing my voice it went 0 for 11. Every judge rated the imitation as human.

obvious AI
caught 11/13 · 0/12 false positives on my real writing
AI in my voice
caught 0/11, the judges rated it human
the twist
a same-author judge rated the fake more me than me, 83.1 vs 82.8

Then I found a signal that seemed to fix it. A cheap deterministic check flipped the score to 11 out of 11 on the imitations, and for an afternoon it looked solved. It wasn't. The check keyed on a punctuation habit the imitation over-applied, and a one-line instruction to the imitator erased it, back to 0 out of 7 on an adaptive version. Worse, a dumb "is this longer than forty words" rule scored identically to my sophisticated one, because the real thing separating the two sets was length, not voice. I'd built a detector for my own test, not for imitation, which is the exact Part 7 lesson committed again by the person who wrote the warning.

So a better detector isn't the answer here. When the imitation is short, in my register, and grounded in things I actually say, there's no signal left in the text, and asking a blind checker "is this John?" is the wrong oracle for the question anyway. What actually holds is provenance, knowing who typed it. The guard still earns its keep, since it catches every lazy imitation for free and never flags my real work, but once a mimic knows the guard exists, where the words came from tells you more than the words do. That ceiling is worth saying out loud, because the failure here isn't missing some mimic in the wild, it's handing a "clean" verdict to the one input built to earn it.

What the Harness Actually Buys

Seven parts have argued one idea, that the harness is the product and the model is a rentable CPU. Here's what that buys, and it lands in three places.

For me and every SE, the harness measurably improved, and it now keeps a change only when it clears a held-out bar, so my own output got better and stopped regressing quietly. It scales past me through the SE Starter Kit, so other SEs can run the same discipline rather than rebuild it. And the next build makes it model-agnostic, the same ~/.claude config ported onto an open runtime so it runs on GPT, Grok, or Gemini, one harness across every model. That port is planned, not shipped, and I'll show it when it runs.

For the customer, every enterprise standing up agents hits the same wall. No way to tell whether a change made the agent better, regressions that ship silently, and "eval" that's really a vibe. What's on this page demonstrates the answer rather than describing it. You measure the change, gate the regression before it reaches anyone, evaluate with a bound instead of a vanity number, and prove where an output came from when no detector can. That's an agent-quality and trust conversation, and it opens a bigger door than a feature demo.

For the platform, all of it maps onto where Agentforce is already heading, across evaluation (Testing Center), auto-improvement (Agent Optimizer), and governance (Agent Fabric). The model-agnostic angle is the BYOLLM promise made concrete. A customer's investment in evals, memory, and governance survives every model swap, because it lives in the harness, above the model layer. The harness is the connective tissue between those surfaces.

You can't own the frontier model. What you can own is the harness, the evals, the memory, and the provenance. Unlike the model you rent, those compound.
